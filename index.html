Client Onboarding, Reimagined

A global investment bank's Prime Brokerage division onboards hedge funds and asset managers through a multi-month process spanning Sales, Credit Risk, KYC, Legal, and Connectivity teams. I mapped how that process actually felt from the inside, then storyboarded a connected system to fix it.

Context & role→ What we found→ The shift→ 5 concept scenes→ Outcome

CONTEXT & ROLE

Why this mattered

Every new client meant a slow, manual re-run of the same onboarding steps across disconnected teams. Clients felt it as confusion and delay; internally, it meant duplicated work and no shared view of progress. My job was to research what was actually happening on the ground, make that visible, then define what "fixed" could look like.

  • Research design & fieldwork

    Planned and ran interviews with clients and internal stakeholders across Sales, Transitions, Credit, KYC, Legal and Connectivity.

  • Research synthesis & blueprint authorship

    Synthesised interviews into a detailed as-is blueprint, mapping front-stage interactions against back-stage systems and an emotional journey curve.

  • Research-led concept design

    Translated research findings into the to-be concept direction, storyboarding it scene-by-scene so both client-facing and internal teams could see how it would feel in practice.

  • Stakeholder alignment

    Helped present the research and the resulting storyboard back to client and internal stakeholders, using it to build alignment around the direction rather than just explain it.

TO-BE · THE SHIFT

One shared system, not five separate ones

Almost every fix traces back to the same move: replace scattered emails, calls and spreadsheets with one place both the client and the bank's teams can see and act on.

01

Progressive profile, not repeated intake

Information is captured once, as early as the first sales conversation, and carried forward automatically.

02

Shared timeline over status calls

A visible tracker replaces "where are we?" calls: everyone sees the same plan, the same dates.

03

Guided, not generic, requests

Every ask tells the client why it's needed and what's still outstanding, instead of 50-document dumps with no context.

Migrating Without Losing Trust

When a global bank acquired another, hundreds of thousands of retail clients had to be migrated onto new accounts, apps, and products, whether they'd asked for it or not. I led the research that uncovered what that actually felt like for them, then turned it into a journey map that aligned Business, IT, and Design around a single, realistic plan.

Context & role→ Research approach→ What we heard→ The journey→ Insight to action→ Outcome

CONTEXT & ROLE

Why this mattered

Some of these clients had never given their bank a second thought since opening their first account decades earlier. Now they had to learn a new app, new products, and a new relationship, on a timeline they didn't choose. My job was to understand what that felt like from their side, then make it concrete enough for Business, IT, and Design to build against together.

  • Research design & fieldwork

    Planned and ran interviews across four client segments, from digitally confident clients to those who'd already been forced to migrate a banking relationship once before.

  • Insight synthesis

    Synthesised interviews and secondary research into five core insights, each paired with concrete communication and product recommendations.

  • Journey mapping

    Authored the end-to-end migration journey, from first notice through to feeling settled, including the emotional curve and flagged risks.

  • Cross-functional alignment

    Used the journey map as the shared reference point that aligned Business, IT, and Design around one realistic plan for delivery.

RESEARCH APPROACH

Who we talked to, and how

Over an eight-week research sprint, we combined client interviews with secondary research, moving from open discovery through to co-creating solutions directly with Business and IT.

WHAT WE HEARD

Five things that shaped the redesign

01

Expectations of transparency

Clients wanted a concrete picture of what would happen and when, not general reassurance.

"Not knowing what was coming next was the hardest part."
02

One consistent message, everywhere

Slightly different messages across letters, app, and branch created more anxiety than the migration itself.

"Three versions of the same letter just left me double-checking which one was right."
03

Experience is bigger than an app

Digital convenience mattered, but many clients still wanted the option of a real conversation.

"I don't mind the new app. I just don't want to lose the person who already knows me."
04

First login is a moment, not the finish line

Clients needed several weeks past activation to feel genuinely settled, especially once a full payment cycle had run cleanly.

"The app worked from day one. Feeling at home took closer to a month."
05

It wasn't their choice, so make it personal

Clients hadn't asked to be migrated, and a one-size-fits-all journey read as tone-deaf to that.

"Nobody asked me if I wanted this. The least you can do is make it easy."

"The app worked from day one. Feeling at home took closer to a month."

A client on the difference between activation and actually settling in

FROM INSIGHT TO ACTION

Three examples of what changed

Every recommendation traced back to a specific client quote. Here are three that shaped the eventual design.

01

Before: one-stop communication

Bundle the timeline, required actions, and access details into a single consistent message repeated across letter, app, and branch, instead of several slightly different versions of the same news.

02

During: show clients their own context

Surface real-time account-balance matching and confirm transferred standing orders during the cutover, so clients aren't left guessing whether anything actually worked.

03

After: take the client by the hand

Offer a guided tour and contextual tips in the days after activation, since the first login is the start of settling in, not the end of the journey.

My contribution

I mapped each pain point to its supporting quote directly, then co-facilitated the workshop that prioritised them with Business and IT.

OUTCOME

What this made possible

The journey map became the shared reference point between Business, IT, and Design, replacing three different mental models of the migration with one realistic, evidence-based plan. It didn't just describe the client experience: it gave delivery teams a concrete brief to build against.

Making Customer Centricity Everyone's Job

A Swiss financial institution's Payment Solutions division wanted every employee, not just designers, to understand and act on what "customer-centric" actually meant for their daily work. I helped lead the research and co-creation behind a CX Vision & Strategy built to do exactly that, and to decide which customers to prioritise first.

Context & role→ Research approach→ What we found→ Prioritisation model→ Vision to strategy→ What I'd do differently→ Outcome

CONTEXT & ROLE

Why this mattered

This followed an earlier CX maturity assessment that surfaced a clear gap: the organisation talked about customer centricity, but had no shared, org-wide definition of what it actually meant or who to prioritise. My job was to help build both, in a way concrete enough that people outside design would actually use it.

  • Research design & fieldwork

    Planned and conducted customer interviews alongside the client's own account managers, combining outside-in customer perspective with inside-out account knowledge.

  • Insight synthesis

    Synthesised interviews into thematic clusters covering trust, expertise, personal relationships, and the quiet influence of third-party software on provider choice.

  • Prioritisation framework

    Co-designed a two-dimensional model that turned a large, undifferentiated customer base into a manageable set of priority archetypes.

  • Vision & strategy development

    Helped translate research into a CX Vision, ten Experience Guidelines, and a strategy meant to read the same way to everyone, regardless of role.

RESEARCH APPROACH

Combining outside-in and inside-out

Working inside an interdisciplinary team split between a "business" perspective and a "human" perspective, we paired direct customer research with an unusually deep look at the client's own internal data.

25interviews (customers & account managers)
10experience guidelines delivered
4core deliverables co-created

Alongside 20 client interviews and 5 conversations with the client's own account managers, we ran an internal analysis of profitability and transaction data across industries, plus an external competitive benchmark of rival providers' experience at key moments like onboarding and decision-making.

WHAT WE FOUND

Trust, expertise, and a quiet third party in every decision

01

Trust built on tradition

Clients associated the institution with deep-rooted, dependable heritage, but expected that trust to be matched with modern relevance.

"You're the bank my parents used. That's a strength, if you can still keep up."
02

Expertise is expected, not optional

Clients wanted a provider who understood their specific industry, not just one that processed payments.

"I don't want to have to explain my own business to the person advising me on it."
03

Personal relationships still win loyalty

A genuine, consistent point of contact mattered more to retention than any single digital feature.

"I'll stay as long as I have someone who actually knows our account."
04

Software vendors quietly decide

For many clients, the choice of payment provider was shaped less by the bank itself than by whichever accounting or ERP software they already used.

"Honestly, we went with whoever our software already talked to."

"You're the bank my parents used. That's a strength, if you can still keep up."

A client reflecting on heritage versus relevance

One vision, understood the same way by everyone

The research fed four deliverables, packaged as a "scrollytelling" interactive walkthrough rather than a deck destined for a shelf. It worked equally well as a guided presentation to leadership and as something any employee could open and explore at their own pace.

WHAT I'D DO DIFFERENTLY

A candid look back

01

Bring the client in as an equal even earlier

Co-creating side by side, and letting the client experience our own working culture directly, gave them real ownership and helped the findings actually travel back into their organisation. Worth leaning into even harder next time.

02

Define roles and accountability sooner

Responsibilities, especially on the client side, could have been clearer from the start. Team culture and informal communication still needed time to develop mid-project.

03

Agree on the final format earlier

Bringing the visual designer in sooner, and settling on the shape of the final deliverable earlier, would have let design and content develop together instead of one waiting on the other.

OUTCOME

What this made possible

The recommendations led directly to a new, dedicated Customer Experience department being created within the client's Payment Solutions division. Presented to senior leadership, the work landed with enough clarity and energy that the client's own team became active advocates for the CX Vision inside the organisation, carrying it forward well beyond the length of the engagement.

Finding Out What Restaurant Owners Actually Want

An industry-wide push to get independent restaurants online assumed owners wanted more digital features. Testing that assumption directly with restaurant owners across three countries showed something more nuanced: most of what gets built for restaurants, restaurants don't actually want.

Context & role→ Research approach→ What we found→ Four owners→ Concept testing→ Outcome

CONTEXT & ROLE

Why this mattered

This was a nine-week student business project for HoReCa.digital, an initiative from Metro Group, who wanted to build a digital menu tool for independent restaurants. My job, alongside a team of five, was to make sure the tool got built around what owners actually wanted, not what seemed technically possible.

  • Research design & fieldwork

    Planned and conducted interviews with independent restaurant owners across Lausanne, Paris, and Munich, alongside focus groups with guests.

  • Design Sprint facilitation

    Followed the Design Sprint methodology to move from insight to eleven testable paper prototypes in a single week.

  • Persona development

    Synthesised interviews into four distinct owner personas, used to decide which features to prioritise for whom.

  • Iterative decision-making

    Made explicit keep, tweak, or kill calls on every prototyped feature based on direct owner feedback, not assumption.

RESEARCH APPROACH

Two rounds of testing, three countries

We paired industry-wide data on restaurant websites with direct fieldwork: paper prototypes tested with owners in round one, a refined interactive prototype tested with owners in round two.

11features paper-prototyped
33restaurant owners interviewed
3countries covered
13guests across two focus groups

Round one tested paper prototypes with owners in Munich and Paris. Based on that feedback, the strongest concepts were rebuilt as an interactive smartphone prototype and tested again with owners in Berlin and Switzerland. In parallel, we analysed 100 restaurant websites in Paris and Berlin against their TripAdvisor and Yelp ratings, to see whether online presence actually correlated with performance.

WHAT WE FOUND

The gap wasn't skill. It was trust.

01

Most menus aren't even online

Close to half the restaurants we analysed in Paris had no menu on their website at all, despite it being the single most-searched-for detail before a visit.

02

PDF menus are a dead end

PDFs force a download, get poorly indexed by search engines, and pull visitors away from the page that's actually meant to sell the restaurant.

03

Photos were more divisive than expected

Both guests and owners, independently, rejected a photo on every dish as something you'd expect from a fast-food menu, not an independent kitchen.

"It seems cheap. It's not our style."
04

The resistance wasn't really about tech skill

Owners' strongest objections were about losing flexibility: a fixed photo or price felt like it would box in a dish they wanted the freedom to keep changing.

"Everyone thinks they are restaurant critics."

An independent restaurant owner on why they're wary of going digital

FOUR OWNERS

Not every owner needed the same tool

Synthesising the interviews surfaced four distinct mindsets, each with a different relationship to going digital.

01

The Dinosaur

20+ years in the business, thriving on reputation and word of mouth. Sees no reason to change what already works.

02

The Skeptical

Struggling to attract guests, aware that digital matters, but not convinced enough to invest time or money in it.

03

The Enthusiast

Understands the value of a strong online presence, but lacks the skills, so hires it out and hopes it's done well.

04

The Early Adopter

Built the business hands-on and wants to run every part of it personally, digital presence included.

Most owners we spoke to fell closest to the Enthusiast: aware of the stakes, but not equipped to act on them alone.

Cybersecurity Insurance, Made Relatable

A Swiss insurance company had already launched a first cyber-security product for individual customers. The next question was bigger: would people actually use a fuller platform to understand and act on their own digital risk, not just insure against it after the fact?

Context & role→ Research approach→ Four archetypes→ What we found→ Guiding principles→ Outcome

CONTEXT & ROLE

Why this mattered

Following a successful first cyber-insurance launch, the brief was to evaluate a much bigger concept: a central hub where people could understand their own digital risk and act on it, not just claim against it afterwards. My job was to get past abstract "cyber security" language and test whether people actually wanted that, and what it would need to earn their trust.

  • Research design & fieldwork

    Planned and conducted ethnographic interviews to understand people's real ambitions, habits, and anxieties around online risk, not just their reaction to a concept.

  • Concept prioritisation exercise

    Designed a "buy-a-feature" exercise, giving participants a symbolic budget to build their own package from the service elements on offer.

  • Archetype development

    Synthesised interviews into four behavioural archetypes, mapped across how much people knew about cyber risk and how actively they acted on it.

  • Principle & concept development

    Helped translate the research into guiding principles and a validated concept for a central, ongoing cyber-security relationship, not a one-off purchase.

RESEARCH APPROACH

Beyond a concept reaction

Twelve in-depth interviews used ethnographic methods to get past surface-level concept reactions, toward how people actually think, feel, and behave around their own online risk.

12ethnographic interviews
3concept elements tested
4behavioural archetypes
3/3hypotheses validated

Alongside the interviews, participants took part in a "buy-a-feature" exercise: given a symbolic budget, they assembled their own package from the available services, showing directly what they valued rather than what they said they valued.

FOUR ARCHETYPES

Not everyone is unaware, and not everyone is careless

Interviews mapped cleanly across two axes: how much people knew about cyber risk, and how actively they did anything about it.

KNOWS THE RISK · PASSIVE

The Comfortable Ones

Have a basic grasp of online risk but under-protect anyway. Open to safety measures, as long as it costs them no real effort.

KNOWS THE RISK · ACTIVE

The Self-Assured

Take calculated risks, trust their own vetted setup over big providers, and often end up informally advising their own circle.

UNAWARE · PASSIVE

The Naive Drifters

The internet is a black box to them. If it's convenient, like free WiFi, they use it without a second thought.

UNAWARE · ACTIVE

The Dependent Ones

Do use protective tools, usually installed by someone else, but don't really understand how they work or why.

The same product pitch does not land the same way with all four. It has to earn attention differently for each.

WHAT WE FOUND

The gap wasn't awareness. It was what to do with it.

01

Everyone feels vaguely at risk, and freezes there

Most people sense a threat is out there but can't picture it concretely enough to act on, or even to know what "safe" would look like.

"I don't feel threatened. I can imagine it's out there, I just don't see it."
02

Trust in insurers is real, but conditional

People already extend baseline trust to insurers on safety and support. That trust doesn't automatically carry over to technical, IT-specific competence.

"I'd trust an insurer with this. More than most other industries."
03

Awareness doesn't reliably lead to action

Even people who know they should do more openly admit they won't, unless it takes close to zero extra effort on their part.

04

Cyber safety spreads person to person, not top-down

People trust their own circle on cyber questions more than official channels, and the more capable ones already act as unofficial IT support for family and friends.

"How do I even prove to an insurer that something happened in the digital world?"

A participant, on why cyber risk feels different from risks they already understand

GUIDING PRINCIPLES

Six principles to keep every decision human

The research converged into six principles, used afterward to keep design and content decisions consistent across teams.

Playful

Approachable enough that people engage before they disengage out of anxiety.

Relatable

Grounded in everyday language and situations, not abstract security jargon.

Educative

Builds real understanding, not just a sense of having "done something."

Actionable

Every insight comes with a next step that's genuinely easy to take.

Curated

A short, well-chosen set of options beats an overwhelming full menu.

Personalised

Tailored to how much a person already knows and does, not a single generic pitch.

OUTCOME

What this made possible

The concept, framed as a central hub for understanding and acting on personal cyber risk, tested well across all four archetypes. All three of the project's core hypotheses were validated directly against the research:

check_circle

People would use a central point of contact for cyber risk, if it were offered by an insurer.

check_circle

Such a platform would genuinely help build their knowledge and awareness of online risk.

check_circle

Customers understood what the hub was offering them.

That gave the client a validated direction, and a concrete set of principles, to carry into the next phase of the offering. A version of this concept, cyber insurance bundled with a broader set of prevention and support services, is now a live product.
